### 2.2 Detailed Defect Register

#### 1. Air Canada Bereavement Fare Chatbot Hallucination (*Moffatt v. Air Canada*)
- **Category:** AI / LLM (Conversational RAG Grounding Failure & Generative Hallucination)
- **Source Link:** [CBC News — Air Canada found liable for chatbot's bad advice on plane tickets](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/air-canada-chatbot-lawsuit-1.7116416) · [Wired — Air Canada Must Pay Passenger After Chatbot Misleads Him](https://www.wired.com/story/air-canada-chatbot-refund-policy/)
- **Date / Publicity Evidence:** February 2024 (Civil Resolution Tribunal decision issued February 14, 2024; global media coverage February 15–20, 2024).
- **Description & Technical Root Cause:** Air Canada deployed an interactive customer service chatbot powered by generative AI without deterministic policy enforcement or schema validation. Passenger Jake Moffatt inquired about bereavement fare discounts following his grandmother's passing. The chatbot provided false advice: *"If you need to travel immediately or have already travelled and would like to submit your ticket for a reduced bereavement rate, kindly do so within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued by completing our Ticket Refund Application form."* In reality, Air Canada's official bereavement tariff strictly prohibited retroactive refunds once travel was completed. The chatbot hallucinated this policy because its retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline lacked schema-bound verification against the airline's static tariff database.
- **Severity:** **Medium** (High legal liability / precedent setting; negligent misrepresentation in consumer contract law without flight-safety implications).
- **Consequences:** Moffatt purchased full-fare flights costing $1,630.36 CAD. Air Canada refused a retroactive refund, arguing before the tribunal that the chatbot was *"a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions."* Civil Resolution Tribunal Member Christopher Rivers rejected this argument, ruling that Air Canada failed to take reasonable care to ensure accuracy and was vicariously liable for its automated systems. The tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay Moffatt **$812.02 CAD** in damages, interest, and fees.
- **Solution / Mitigation:** Air Canada disabled the generative conversational chatbot component; replaced customer-facing generative bots with deterministic FAQ decision trees and explicit human agent escalation pathways.

- **Source Link:** [OpenAI Official Technical Incident Post-Mortem](https://openai.com/index/march-20-chatgpt-outage/)
- **Date / Publicity Evidence:** March 20, 2023 (Incident occurred March 20, 2023; technical post-mortem published March 24, 2023).
- **Description & Technical Root Cause:** OpenAI used `asyncio` and the open-source `redis-py` library to interface with Redis Cluster caching user profiles and session states. In `redis-py`, requests and responses operated via two queues across a pooled connection set. When an async request was cancelled (e.g., client timeout or network disconnect) after pushing to the incoming queue but before reading from the outgoing queue, the connection was returned to the pool in a contaminated state without flushing the socket buffer. - **Solution / Mitigation:** OpenAI patched `redis-py` to ensure cancelled async requests actively terminate or purge dirty socket buffers rather than recycling them; decoupled session authentication tokens from the asynchronous cache pipeline; added secondary assertions verifying that returned cache session IDs match the authenticated requester's UID.

#### 19. Atlassian Confluence Broken Access Control Setup Wizard Zero-Day (CVE-2023-22515)
- **Category:** General / Web Application Authorization (Broken Access Control State Reset)
- **Source Link:** [NIST NVD CVE-2023-22515](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2023-22515)
- **Date / Publicity Evidence:** October 4, 2023 (Advisory issued October 4, 2023; updated with active exploitation alerts October 10–16, 2023).
- **Description & Technical Root Cause:** Broken access control and unauthenticated state reset flaw in Confluence Data Center and Server. An architectural flaw in the Struts/XWork action mappings allowed unauthenticated remote attackers to send an HTTP GET or POST request to `/setup/setupadministrator.action` or `/setup/finishsetup.action`. The endpoint failed to check whether the application was already initialized, allowing an attacker to reset the setup status flag, trigger the initial administrative setup wizard, and create a brand-new administrator account with full root privileges without supplying existing credentials.
- **Severity:** **Critical** (CVSS v3.1: 10.0 / CVE-2023-22515 / Pre-authentication administrative takeover).
- **Consequences:** Extensively exploited in the wild by threat actors, including state-sponsored groups and ransomware operators (such as Storm-0062), to establish persistent administrator access, exfiltrate confidential enterprise wikis and intellectual property, and deploy ransomware.
- **Solution / Mitigation:** Atlassian issued hotfixes in Confluence versions 8.3.3, 8.4.3, 8.5.2, etc., that strictly block access to `/setup/*` endpoints once initial installation is finalized; recommended immediate interim mitigation by blocking incoming HTTP traffic matching `/setup/*` at edge reverse proxies and WAFs.

#### 20. Spring4Shell (Spring Framework Core Remote Code Execution, CVE-2022-22965)
- **Category:** General / Application Framework (Insecure Property Binding & ClassLoader Traversal)
- **Source Link:** [VMware Spring Security Advisory](https://spring.io/blog/2022/03/31/spring-framework-rce-early-announcement)
- **Date / Publicity Evidence:** March 31, 2022 (Public leak March 30, official VMware advisory March 31, 2022; widespread exploitation April 2022).
- **Description & Technical Root Cause:** Insecure property binding and ClassLoader traversal in Spring Framework's `DataBinder`. When a Spring MVC application handled HTTP POST requests, `DataBinder` automatically mapped query parameters to Java object fields. Under Java 9+ (which introduced the Java Module System), accessing `class.module.classLoader` became possible via bean property reflection. An unauthenticated attacker could craft an HTTP request that traversed the ClassLoader hierarchy into Apache Tomcat's `AccessLogValve` logger (e.g., `class.module.classLoader.resources.context.parent.pipeline.first.pattern`), modifying the log directory to the webroot, altering the log file extension to `.jsp`, and passing malicious Java code inside the log format string, causing Tomcat to write an arbitrary JSP webshell directly to the server.
- **Severity:** **Critical** (CVSS v3.1: 9.8 / CVE-2022-22965 / Unauthenticated Remote Code Execution).
- **Consequences:** Wide-scale zero-day panic across enterprise Java ecosystems, mirroring Log4Shell concerns; hundreds of web applications were compromised by automated botnets (such as Mirai) to deploy cryptocurrency miners and persistent reverse shells.
- **Solution / Mitigation:** Spring Framework released versions 5.3.18 and 5.2.20, adding a strict denylist and property-name allowlist filter in `CachedIntrospectionResults.java` that explicitly restricts property binding to `ClassLoader` and `ProtectionDomain` accessors; recommended updating Tomcat and running applications on modern embedded servlet containers (like Spring Boot executable JARs) where Tomcat's `AccessLogValve` was not vulnerable to traversal.

### 3.2 CLO G9.3 — AI Test Case Analysis & Missed Edge Cases (Bloom Level: Analyze)

When I asked AI models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) to generate test cases for a smart desk lamp, they almost exclusively focused on **phone app features**:
* Turning the lamp on/off using the app button.
* Dragging the brightness slider in the app.
* Setting a schedule (e.g., turn off at 11 PM).
* Testing wrong Wi-Fi passwords.
* Testing voice commands ("Hey Google, turn on lamp").

#### Why Does AI Miss Physical Hardware Bugs?
AI models think like software developers testing a mobile screen or a web API. They don't have a physical body, so they forget that a real household lamp deals with real-world physics: loose electrical plugs, heat buildup, physical hinges getting loose over time, and eye-straining light flicker.

To fulfill the **CLO G9.3 (Analyze)** requirement, I identified **5 critical physical edge cases** that the AI completely overlooked:

| # | Missed Physical Edge Case | What AI Overlooks | Why This Test Matters in Real Life |
|:---:|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Wiggling / Loose Power Plug (<200ms rapid disconnects)** | AI assumes power is either 100% ON or 100% OFF. It forgets that cords get kicked or wall plugs get loose. | When a plug wiggles, power cuts on and off several times in a split second. A good device must reset cleanly without freezing, glitching, or damaging its internal chips. |
| **2** | **Pushing Down and Vigorously Spinning the Knob Past the Limit** | AI assumes users turn knobs gently. It doesn't test violent, rapid inputs or pressing while spinning past the maximum stop. | Rapid spinning can confuse the knob's internal sensor. If the code isn't guarded, the color temperature could glitch, wrap from cool white back to yellow, or lock up the controls. |
| **3** | **1 Hour at 100% Brightness (Heat & Arm Sagging Test)** | AI doesn't understand thermodynamics or gravity. It assumes the lamp arm stays in place forever. | LEDs get warm. If the lamp runs at full power for 60 minutes, the heat can warm up the metal hinge and soften its friction pads, causing the heavy arm to slowly droop down. |
| **4** | **Invisible Light Flicker (Slow-Motion 240fps Camera Test)** | AI assumes that if light shines, quality is fine. It cannot "see" high-speed optical flicker. | Cheap or poorly regulated LED lights flicker hundreds of times per second. Even if invisible to the eye, this causes eye strain and headaches. A slow-motion camera check verifies steady, eye-safe light (IEEE 1789 standard). |
| **5** | **Can the Lamp Be Waterproof? (Accidental Desk Liquid Spill & IP20 Safety)** | AI never asks if household electronics can handle water. It assumes the lamp is operated in a sterile, dry virtual environment. | Desk lamps sit right next to drinking glasses and coffee mugs. The Xiaomi lamp has an **IP20 rating** (dry indoor use only — **not waterproof**). If water spills near the base or knob, does it seep into the internal PCB, and does the 12V adapter safely shut down without causing a short circuit, smoke, or electric shock? |

## 5. AI Critique (227 words)

AI models often make mistakes, hallucinate, or show bias when we do not give them enough clear instructions. This experience shows that we cannot trust AI blindly. We must use AI more strictly by providing detailed context, clear constraints, and the exact format we expect. Most importantly, testers must always double-check and verify every answer generated by AI before using it.
